Pare the most painful process

Then ask the sharper question: should this process exist?

Parsync · October 2026

When a workflow keeps hurting, the default response is to buy software. The cheaper response, and the one most vendors skip, is to map the pain first and remove what should not be there. Paring is how you earn the right to automate.

Every growing operation accumulates process debt the same way it accumulates tools: one urgent fix at a time. A spreadsheet because the CRM would not flex. A weekly standup because handoffs kept slipping. A double entry step because two departments never agreed on one system of record. None of it felt like a “process” when it was invented. Together it is the reason your team is tired.

Pare the most painful process means you do not start with the easiest win or the loudest executive request. You start with the workflow where delay, error, or rework shows up in customer experience, revenue, or compliance. You document it with the people who live in it. Then you cut.

How to pick the workflow

Use pain that repeats on a clock, not pain from a one-off project. Good signals:

  • The same exception gets escalated every week.
  • Two systems disagree and a human reconciles them manually.
  • New hires need a tribal-knowledge tour to do the job.
  • You have already bought a tool for this area and adoption stalled.

That workflow becomes your process audit candidate. One chain, end to end. If you need a forcing function, ask: “Where would an extra hire go first?” That is often the painful process in disguise.

Map before you measure ROI

Teams rush to ROI models before they agree on steps. Map first:

  • Trigger and done definition (what counts as finished?).
  • Each step, owner, and system touched.
  • Wait time versus touch time (where does work sit idle?).
  • Quality checks: where do errors get caught, and at what cost?

You are not optimizing yet. You are making the invisible visible. When leaders see duplicate paths, the conversation changes from “we need AI” to “why do we do this twice?”

Pare: remove steps that fail the existence test

Paring is deliberate removal. For each step, ask:

  • If we stopped doing this, would a customer, auditor, or partner notice?
  • Does this step fix a problem we could eliminate upstream?
  • Is this reporting for a meeting that no longer decides anything?
  • Are we copying data the next system could read directly?

Steps that fail those questions are candidates to delete, merge, or replace with a single source of truth. This is where money is saved before any license fee. It is also where trust is built with staff, because you are not automating their workaround; you are removing the reason the workaround existed.

Should this process exist?

Some workflows should not exist in their current form at all. They are compensating for a product decision, a policy nobody owns, or a handoff between teams that should be one team. Others should exist but are overloaded: too many approvals, too many formats, too many channels into the same queue.

Asking “should this process exist?” is not nihilism. It is scope control. If the answer is “yes, but not like this,” you redesign. If the answer is “only because we never integrated X and Y,” you fix the integration or the org boundary. If the answer is “no, but we’re required to,” you minimize and automate the proof, not the fantasy of value.

Skipping this question is how teams fund automation theater: expensive motion with the same outcomes.

What to keep human

Paring is not “fire everyone and buy bots.” Some steps stay human because stakes are high: clinical judgement, contract language, brand voice on customer-facing sends, or anything where a wrong automatic action is expensive. Mark those as human-in-the-loop before you sync tools. Automation should prepare the packet; people should confirm the decision.

After paring, sync

What survives paring is smaller and honest. That is the right surface for sync: integrations, an AI client with the right access, or straightforward automation where the rules are stable. If you are evaluating AI vendors before paring, you are paying someone to encode chaos.
